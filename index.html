Version control for writing

Writ replaces the single shared draft with branches. Everyone writes their version in peace, then merges. A semantic merge reads what each edit means rather than which characters changed — so tidying merges on its own, and a change of meaning stops and waits for a person.

The problem

A shared document has one copy and everybody types into it. That is fine for three people and an afternoon. Past that it fails in a specific way: two people edit the same sentence, the later keystroke wins, and nobody can see that a decision was made. There is no record, because nothing was ever proposed — it was just typed.

The usual repair is worse than the fault. People stop making the edit they believe in and make the edit nobody will object to. The document converges on whatever survives contact with the most people, which is rarely the sharpest version of anything.

What Writ does

Writ gives every writer a branch. You edit your own copy, at your own pace, without racing anyone. When you are ready, you merge.

The merge is the interesting part. Writ compares what your edit means against what the document already said, rather than which characters moved. Two outcomes:

If the meaning is unchanged — you fixed a typo, cut a redundant clause, swapped a word for a better one — it is wording, and it merges without asking anyone. Nobody needs to review a comma.

If the meaning changed, it is a proposal, not a fact. The edit stops, the document keeps what it said, and a named person has to approve it or block it. Disagreements become visible and attributable instead of disappearing into an edit history nobody reads.

“60 minutes or more” → “more than 60 minutes” Four characters, and it looks like a tidy-up. It removes exactly one case — a delay of precisely sixty minutes — and that case is somebody's refund. A character diff cannot tell you that. Reading for meaning can.

Conflicts resolve once and merge for everyone. A branch that never merges is not a failure: it can keep going and become its own document. And because every change was proposed before it landed, the document's whole history stays readable on one map.
